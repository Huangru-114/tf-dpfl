## 6.1.x Why the edge-shared block protects: isolation of transmission

Under concentrated placement, keeping the last residual block within each edge leaves the victim edges with an attack success rate of 0.27–0.41 at cloud round 60 (0.28 averaged over the last ten evaluations, Section 6.1). Two explanations fit this number. Either only part of the backdoor function reaches the victim edges, or the backdoor does reach them but the evaluation trigger no longer works there: its adversarial-noise component is optimised on the attacker's evaluation personalized model, whose last block now differs from that of the victims. The two explanations call for different readings of the defense, so we separated them on model snapshots saved at cloud round 60, without any further training.

For every benign client in the victim edges we built the trigger twice on the same probe images, with the same random start and the same generator perturbation: once with the noise optimised on the attacker's model (the evaluation trigger used throughout this thesis) and once on the client's own evaluation personalized model (a white-box trigger). If the trigger merely failed to transfer, the white-box trigger would recover a much higher attack success rate. We fixed the reading in advance: a white-box rate at least 0.30 above the evaluation rate and at least 0.5 in every seed would mean "the backdoor is present but the trigger does not transfer"; a difference below 0.10 in every seed would mean "the backdoor was not transmitted".

The white-box trigger is not more effective (Figure 6.1b). With the edge-shared block it reaches 0.33, 0.26 and 0.34 in the three seeds, against 0.40, 0.27 and 0.41 for the evaluation trigger, a difference of −0.07, −0.01 and −0.07. Without the defense both triggers reach about 1.0. The decomposition in the same figure points the same way: without the defense the generator perturbation alone already succeeds on 0.998–1.000 of the probe images, whereas with the edge-shared block it succeeds on 0.21–0.24. What remains in the victim edges is the part of the backdoor that crossed the edge boundary; the edge-shared block limits the transmission of the backdoor rather than hiding its trigger. [Limits: measured at cloud round 60 under concentrated placement only; the white-box noise uses the same single-step budget as the evaluation trigger, and a stronger white-box search might find more.]

A natural reading of this result is that the backdoor resides mainly in the last residual block, which the edge-shared block keeps local. We could not verify this premise: retraining the compromised edge on clean data either left the backdoor intact or removed it only at the price of a large loss of clean accuracy (Appendix A).

## 6.3.x Direct evidence for the feedback loop

Section 6.3.y showed that, once filtering is switched on, the attackers' suspicion scores fall to the level of benign clients and then follow the backdoor strength of the compromised edge (Figure 6.7). Our explanation was that a bounded trigger changes the internal statistics of a model only after the model has learned the backdoor, so that the reference score measures whether a model already carries a backdoor rather than whether a client is poisoning. We tested this directly on snapshots: three settings (concentrated and dispersed placement without defense, and concentrated placement with the edge-shared block), three seeds, cloud rounds 6, 15 and 60, and four edges each, 108 edge models in total. For each edge model we fed the edge's 500 clean images with and without the trigger through one training-mode forward pass and measured how much the trigger shifts the inputs of every batch-normalisation layer (a per-channel Gaussian divergence, averaged over layers). We related this shift to the backdoor strength of the same edge, the median trigger margin of its benign clients recorded during training.

The two quantities move together (Figure 6.7b). The rank correlation is 0.91 over all 108 edge models and 0.90–0.93 within each seed; none of 10,000 permutations reached the observed value (p = 0.0001, the smallest value this test can report). Within each training round the correlation is 0.78–0.89, and a permutation that only shuffles within rounds again gives p = 0.0001, so the association is not an artefact of both quantities growing with training. This meets the criterion we fixed in advance (pooled correlation at least 0.5, positive within every seed, p < 0.01), and we therefore regard the feedback loop as supported by moderate rather than weak evidence. The evidence remains correlational. The shift is measured on the edge backbone in training mode, the strength on the clients' personalized models (the two share the same backbone), and the four edges of one run share one trigger generator, so the 108 units are not fully independent.

The implication for defense design is that a filter of this kind can at best hold the backdoor at an equilibrium: suppressing the backdoor makes the attacker look benign, and readmitting the attacker lets the backdoor grow until the score rises again. Tuning the threshold moves the equilibrium but cannot remove it.

## Appendix A. Retraining probe

**Purpose.** Section 6.1 suggests that the edge-shared block works because the backdoor lives mainly in the last residual block. We tried to test this by retraining parts of the compromised edge's model on clean data and measuring how much of the backdoor disappears.

**Method.** On snapshots of the undefended run under concentrated placement (three seeds, cloud rounds 15 and 60) we retrained the compromised edge's model on the edge's 500 clean images with stochastic gradient descent and a batch size of 32, updating either the last residual block, all layers before it, or the whole backbone. Because the clients keep private batch-normalisation statistics, every client of that edge re-estimated its statistics on its own training split after retraining (an exact average of batch statistics, without augmentation); the baseline without retraining went through the same re-estimation. We report the share of the backdoor removed, (ASR before − ASR after) / (ASR before − trigger floor), where the trigger floor is the attack success rate of the same trigger in a run without poisoning, and the clean-accuracy cost, both for the benign clients of the compromised edge. Before any probe we re-ran the original evaluation on each snapshot; the recomputed attack success rates differed from the recorded ones by at most 0.0005.

**First probe.** With the retraining budget of our earlier pre-upload hardening screen (learning rate one tenth of the clients' rate at that round, 75 steps, a temporary linear head fitted on clean features), none of the three variants removed anything: all 18 removed shares are 0.000 (largest 0.0006), and the largest change of any weight was only 0.0003–0.014, depending on the variant and the round. Retraining the whole backbone, our positive control, failed as well, so this probe cannot tell where the backdoor resides; the label "mainly in earlier layers" produced by the pre-specified rule is not meaningful here.

**Follow-up probe.** We then designed a stronger probe (after seeing the first result; its rules were written down before it ran). On one seed and the whole backbone only, we tried twelve settings: constant learning rates of 0.01, 0.03 and 0.1, 75 or 300 steps, and either the fitted linear head or a re-initialised one. A setting qualified if it removed at least half of the backdoor at both rounds with a clean-accuracy cost of at most 0.05; the smallest qualifying setting would then have been used to compare the three variants. No setting qualified (Figure A.1). With learning rates up to 0.03 the removed share stays at most 0.013. With a learning rate of 0.1 and the fitted head, 0.70–0.99 of the backdoor disappears, but the clean accuracy of the benign clients drops by 0.18–0.43 (from 0.84 to 0.42 at round 15). With the re-initialised head, at most 0.18 is removed at a cost of 0.06–0.10.

**Interpretation.** Within this range, clean fine-tuning removes the backdoor only together with the model's usefulness, so the probe cannot localise it, and the premise that the backdoor resides in the last block remains unverified. Part of the accuracy loss may come from evaluating the retrained backbone with the clients' private heads, which were not retrained; we therefore do not read this result as evidence that the backdoor is entangled with the main task.

**Side observation.** The private batch-normalisation statistics stored in the snapshots are stale. Re-estimating them alone raised the clean accuracy of the compromised edge's benign clients by 0.075–0.14 at round 15, which suggests that the personalized accuracies reported in the main text are conservative.
